At two concerts this weekend, something interesting happened. I've been to hundreds of shows and I've maybe seen this once. Both artists, right before their biggest song, told everyone to put their phones away to enjoy the moment. 

These two artists, Florence + the Machine and The Last Dinner Party, have a lot to do with each other musically but come from different generations. However, before playing Nothing Matters for the latter and Dog Days Are Over for the former - the song of my generation - they asked everyone to be in the moment. 

Shockingly, outside one or two dissenters, everyone obliged (I hope, happily). Florence specifically said that you come to her show for that song and that if you're filming, she mimed holding a phone up, that you can't be dancing since you wouldn't want to ruin your shot. 

For a bit now, there have been plenty of news coverage and features about a rebellion against the over-digitalization of our world. From Gen Z's embrace of early-aughts era digital cameras to resurgence of purchasing vinyl. While you may never again watch Sabrina Carpenter at Coachella not perform to a sea of iPhones, I think the experience this weekend wasn't a coincidence.

I bring up Carpenter not just because I'm a fan but because it was telling how much the crowd at her show got flack. Frankly, rightfully. Those are hard tickets to get. They're expensive, inconvenient to get to and many people there probably spent the day standing in the same spot just to be that close when she performed. 

Kyle Chayka's [piece on taste](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/infinite-scroll/why-tech-bros-are-now-obsessed-with-taste) being the next defining attribute as we enter an age of AI hasn't stopped rolling around my head since he published it. Fundamentally, how do you develop taste if you only experience the world through the same cracked screen you experience everything else on? I hope for those that were banking on getting the Instagram story of the night for those songs, that they then experienced something else. That while they won't have the digital proof that they remember how the lead singer of TLDP danced, how she used the brilliant set design to her advantage, or how it felt to be there with a loved one. For me, I won't forget what it was like to finally scream out a song in person that I've listened to hundreds of times in my AirPods

I spend a lot of time talking to tech companies - it is quite literally my day job. My whole career, I have tried to emphasize the importance of centering the human experience when talking about technology. People don't care about the spec sheets as much as they care what the technology will do for them, how it'll make them feel, and how it'll make their lives better. I'll spare you the 'what this pop concert means for B2B tech marketing' take — but come on.

Hell, I'm writing this while waiting to get into a room where my company and I are hosting an in-person event. While I'm sucking down coffee because it's before 7 am and I've been up for hours already - it excites me to be here with people. And, if our events go like they usually do, we won't see a lot of phones.
